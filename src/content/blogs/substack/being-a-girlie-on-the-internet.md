I&#8217;m doing an experiment called being unironically online. The goal is to self-publish randomly and chaotically until <em>someone</em> notices me or my art and decides to write about me with poise. The result would be either fame or embarrassment. The process mostly involves turning my practices into internet contents, and framing my identity crisis as an internet performance.</p><p>There&#8217;s something that feels inherently feminine in making work about <em>putting oneself online</em>. From artists and writers <a href="https://mollysoda.substack.com/">Molly Soda</a>, <a href="https://mirandajuly.com/">Miranda July</a>, <a href="https://internetprincess.substack.com/">Internet Princess</a>, <a href="https://www.instagram.com/mackenzie/">Mackenzie Thomas</a>, and <a href="https://www.instagram.com/mayaontheinternet/">Maya Man</a> whose contents I frequently enjoy, to the overwhelming girl blog / dinner / math trends that feel either too meta or slightly questionable on the grand scheme of cyber-feminism, it seems like the strength and vulnerability of displaying femininity (and being &#10024;unapologetically #girl&#10024;) is weaving the web around us last year. </p><p>Being online is about being relatable but still unique somehow. In the cracks of consuming and creating in the <em>cyberspace</em> I&#8217;m experiencing oscillating struggles relating to my femininity, entangled with a deep uneasiness existing on (western) social media, having a hard time relating to either (mostly) white girl bloggers or Asian (American) girl writers. </p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><img src="/images/blogs/substack/being-a-girlie-on-the-internet/31a6b87f-9592-482e-afd0-0c6ae8d57817_1125x1290.jpeg" alt="" loading="lazy" ><figcaption class="image-caption">*reposts* me fr</figcaption></figure></div><p>Growing up in a non-western culture while consuming so much American media you&#8217;d really think you know this<em> foreign country</em> so well, until you&#8217;re <em>actually</em> here turning your entire life around. And 9 years later you still hold a specific fear that you&#8217;re not &#8220;American enough&#8221; to be on Twitter or even engage in most online discourses. Is this even relatable at all?</p><p>Growing up in an environment that lacked critical theory I learned that feminist wisdom was amongst the wise white women. I related to Joan Didion, Virginia Woolf, Emily Dickinson, and later to Phoebe Waller-Bridge&#8217;s Fleabag, Greta Gerwig&#8217;s Jo March, Sally Rooney&#8217;s <a href="https://internetprincess.substack.com/p/standing-on-the-shoulders-of-complex">complicated female characters</a>&#8230; I held tightly onto my upper-middle class white women heroes because I didn&#8217;t know any better (and didn&#8217;t know Audre Lorde), but also because of the general relatability of womanhood presented by them, through cultural discourses, to me.</p><p>But then I learned there were also Cathy Park Hong, Rina Sawayama, Celest Ng, Ocean Vuong, Michelle Zauner <em>Crying in H Mart</em> &#8212; femme and queer Asians discussing heritage and displacement with brutal honesty. Their stories, and the dialogs around them, immediately resonated better. I experience the same fear and anger towards anti-Asian hate crimes, face the same objectification, struggle with the same internalized racism, feel the same weight and complexity and pride over Crazy Rich Asians, Beef, Everything Everywhere All at Once represented in media. But it&#8217;s different. It&#8217;s not the same at all. </p><p>&#8220;Home&#8221; isn&#8217;t a nostalgic landscape. </p><p>Language isn&#8217;t the bridge of lineage and acceptance. </p><p>Immigration isn&#8217;t a complex story of generational trauma. </p><p>My broken passport and its 539463254 pages of VISA stamps weigh me down every single day.</p><p>I published a <a href="https://www.instagram.com/p/Czk7-jkucEH/?img_index=1">comic</a> recently that tried to explain what my diaspora meant for me in light of a massive movement back home, and the way it influenced how I &#8212; we &#8212; engaged with Chinese politics overseas. It took me a whole year to put how I felt into words. I didn&#8217;t know how. It was not an Asian American experience, and not at all a Chinese experience either. I felt paralyzed, empowered, relieved, misunderstood, for being away in a place that I in fact call home, crying about the other place that I call home. I typed these words into my phone winter of 2023, and I&#8217;m still seeking the same answer now:</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><img src="/images/blogs/substack/being-a-girlie-on-the-internet/7ebf7045-3b86-4ccd-b273-f44690cf874a_2000x2000.jpeg" alt="" loading="lazy" ></figure></div><p>Sometimes I feel like I&#8217;m trying to weave an immigration story intertwined with queerness and femme-ness and <em>learning to radicalize</em> that is so fundamentally unrelatable to the <em>general</em> public, and if I can&#8217;t fight it I&#8217;d just have to swallow it.</p><p>Sometimes I yearn for a look into the future, where this diaspora has already become a language of its own, a narrative well-paved for me, presented and packaged beautifully and served in plates as art or memes. .</p><p>Sometimes I wonder if I would&#8217;ve felt a little more coherent if I simply <em>be</em> an internet artist so that <em>I </em>could just embody it all, floating in the <em>cyberspace </em>the way I float as a cultural refugee.</p><p>And I&#8217;ll eat my girl dinner there.</p><p>More about this soon.</p><div><hr></div><p>On to happy things!
