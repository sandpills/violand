<p><strong>Wednesday, Aug 30. </strong></p><p>I laid in the grass, in a slightly uncomfortable position, waiting for 3 PM to arrive, sunlight spreading evenly and generously on my body. I was half asleep, thinking about how my body always knows when something is about to come to an end. </p><p>The cicadas screamed loudly in response, upset about the summer ending. Impending changes dwelled inside me, lingering, circling around, while I try my hardest to close my eyes and &#8220;live in the moment&#8221;. Everything here had been so bight &#8212; Too bright. Too soft. too quiet too nice too beautiful too easy toogoodtobetrue</p><p>This is going to be a long one, please bear with me.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><img src="/images/blogs/substack/the-answer-may-or-may-not-be-in-the/2590a904-7b9f-464c-8133-81acc72b7d6a_2663x1997.jpeg" alt="" loading="lazy" ><figcaption class="image-caption">wave farm, end of summer</figcaption></figure></div><p>It was the second to last day of my residency at <a href="https://wavefarm.org/">Wave Farm</a>, a transmissions arts center and FM radio station in the Upper Hudson Valley. I had already done everything I came here to do &#8212; worked on my <a href="https://wavefarm.org/wf/calendar/jnsrtg">project</a>, livestreamed an<a href="https://soundcloud.com/v10101a/livecoding-on-wgxc-907-fm-audio-buffet"> hour-long livecoded set</a> on the radio, pre-recorded not <a href="https://wavefarm.org/wf/archive/1g7d0b">one</a>, but <a href="https://wavefarm.org/radio/wgxc/audio-archive/t46rjk">two</a> interviews with amazing people, spent time with every installation, even took a little stroll into the woods after dark to conquer my fear&#8230; and yet, uneasiness float to the surface of this quietness I&#8217;ve experienced all week. A small yet growing balloon of anxiety waits to be released.</p><p>Time had already started to warp in funny ways a couple days prior. I could feel the changes clearly.  Memories had started to blur, while daylight gradually slipped through my finger tips. For pockets of time I got taken back to NYC as I spent long nights alone, at the residency, re-working tracks for my radio set. All the associated feelings rolled onto shore &#8212; the anxiety, the excitement, the tears I cried over the past months, the impending doom that&#8217;s waiting for me in Brooklyn, the unresolved tension and conflicts, the events piling up on my calendar, the work left untouched, the time ticking, the love, hate, jealousy, thrill, pain &#8212; everything that drove me to the edge and back. Everything that pushed me forward to where I was, a room in the woods, in a moment of brief <em><strong>silence</strong></em>.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><img src="/images/blogs/substack/the-answer-may-or-may-not-be-in-the/cea4f938-2e35-4799-9ea2-83f3a4e2403e_4032x3024.jpeg" alt="" loading="lazy" ><figcaption class="image-caption">a room of one&#8217;s own</figcaption></figure></div><p><strong>July, at some point</strong></p><p>One sad night a month back, during one of my hyper-fixated deep-dive sessions, the almighty YouTube algorithm bestowed this gem of a video essay to me, called <em>The Answer is Not a Hut in the Woods</em>:</p><div id="youtube2-PK2SMIOHYig" class="youtube-wrap" data-attrs="{&quot;videoId&quot;:&quot;PK2SMIOHYig&quot;,&quot;startTime&quot;:null,&quot;endTime&quot;:null}" data-component-name="Youtube2ToDOM"><div class="youtube-inner"><iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/PK2SMIOHYig?rel=0&amp;autoplay=0&amp;showinfo=0&amp;enablejsapi=0" frameborder="0" loading="lazy" gesture="media" allow="autoplay; fullscreen" allowautoplay="true" allowfullscreen="true" width="728" height="409"></iframe></div></div><p>For the next 46 minutes, I listened to this sad British writer man talk about his silly, younger years looking for meaning in the wild, working in a farm in New Zealand one summer, and then years later deciding to hike the Appalachian Trial like all other sad Europeans attempting to <em>*find themselves*</em>. Across continents and years of time, through encounters, murmurs, doubts, adventures, and lots of reflections on his nuanced feelings, the message he reached at the end was, well, <em>the answer is not a hut in the woods </em>(but is the friends we made along the way or something).</p><p>I found myself crying, lying face up on the dirty living room rug and thinking to myself: &#8220;I guess I need to hike the Appalachian Trial NOW.&#8221;</p><p>So I packed my bags-</p><p>&#8230;lol jk. I&#8217;m not a sad British writer man on a self discovery journey. The urge to drop everything for the idea of a hermit Shangri-La does not exist in the dreams of anxious 27 year-old Chinese artists stuck in VISA hell and can be deported at any time. I need to work. I need to prove my worth just to continue staying here. I can&#8217;t afford to even look for answers. Also I know &#8212; we all know &#8212; that there&#8217;s no answer. No magical equation to solve all of our mental health squabbles. No amount of seclusion or self-inflicted pain or whatever that turns our brain into a whirling mess will make sense of us, the complex social beings that we are, struggling to understand ourselves and each other. And I know that.</p><p>If only it was all that simple.</p><p><strong>Quiet, and </strong></p><p><em>Quiet, and Sometimes Silent</em><strong> </strong>is the name of my project that brought me to Wave Farm. It was about proximity, intimacy, and subtle communication using systems outside centralized infrastructures &#8212; re-imagining connection with people around us with small data packets that are sporadic, temporal, fleeting, non-invasive.</p><p>I had been exploring <a href="https://www.thethingsnetwork.org/docs/lorawan/what-is-lorawan/">LoRa protocol</a> since working on my grad school thesis, as it sits on hobbyist radio frequencies (some hundred megahertz) that are free to use and relatively <em>quiet</em>, comparing to the more commonly utilized protocols like Bluetooth and WiFi on the higher 2.4G frequency. </p><p>Now that a year has passed since my first attempt, this prototype version finally feels like it&#8217;s working as intended: a pair of battery-operated, pocket-size transceivers that send and receive 1) signal strength, 2) rotational information to and from each other, a couple hundred meters apart.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><img src="/images/blogs/substack/the-answer-may-or-may-not-be-in-the/53aa4506-69c1-43e7-bce8-a4503c7aacfa_4000x2668.jpeg" alt="" loading="lazy" ><figcaption class="image-caption">ashamed to have people hold exposed breadboards | photo by Alon Koppel</figcaption></figure></div><p>2 days upon my arrival at Wave Farm, artists <a href="https://mayukofujino.com/">Mayuko Fujino</a><strong> </strong>and<strong> </strong><a href="https://meredithkooi.us/">Meredith Kooi</a> came to do 1-on-1 sessions with me, an excuse to range test the devices as well as inviting others to perform this experience. </p><p>I wasn&#8217;t fully ready. I had Mayuko hold the device and walk into the woods, with very vague intentions explained badly. &#8220;Press the button if you want to, and I&#8217;ll do the same&#8221;, I said. There&#8217;s only one button and one screen on each device. Whenever the small yellow LED sparks on my end, I know she&#8217;d pressed the button. </p><p>She left my sight quickly and was gone for a long time. Whenever the data stopped coming I wondered what she was doing &#8212; was she out of range, or did something else catch her attention (later she told me she was looking at birds)? And sometimes I sent her messages just to make sure she was still there. Sometimes she responded, other times she didn&#8217;t. There was not much to do except for pressing one button while waiting anxiously.</p><p>She explained, in the best way possible, how the experience connected to her as a form of passive communication. Like occasionally checking in with friends and family but not really talk that much, or at all. Like when G-chat used to be a tiny window attached to G-mail, where you could see green dots light up in the peripheral view as an indication of your contacts being online.&nbsp;&#8220;Just knowing you were on the other side felt like reassurance,&#8221; she said. Is it an introvert thing, or is it universal? We talked about the power of the <em><strong>silent knowing</strong></em>. I was surprised and excited how much could be conveyed through the little buttons at all. </p><p>I often think about how we&#8217;re small creatures existing on a massive electromagnetic spectrum, surrounded by waves of various length despite not seeing or hearing most of them. <em>Quiet</em><strong> </strong>in this sense is like being alone in the woods during these summer nights, where cicadas scream and owls cry so loudly, reletlessly. Yet it still feels like darkness. Silence. Just a small human in a field &#8212; until the small yellow LED blinks at me.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><img src="/images/blogs/substack/the-answer-may-or-may-not-be-in-the/0b3ec988-fe60-4388-9077-72431d33fdb2_3200x4000.jpeg" alt="" loading="lazy" ><figcaption class="image-caption">taking computer on a walk | photo by Alon Koppel</figcaption></figure></div><p>Originally I had also planned to have wearable pieces go with the electronics, small mechanisms that flap and fold as the communication happens. I found out half way through that I was missing transistors, which I couldn&#8217;t get there. The wearable parts remained standalone prototypes.</p><p>It&#8217;s so hard to view my projects unironically when they&#8217;re incomplete, if not with major sentiments of shame or horror. Oftentimes this is how it is, especially with the intention to pursue art opportunities. There&#8217;s no end to the performance anxiety, only jokes and giggles to offset the insecurity. When my friend <a href="http://www.johanndiedrick.com/">Johann</a> came visit one afternoon, I told him I was feeling emotional how people at Wave Farm even remotely cared about me and my work, let alone with so much good faith and enthusiasm. &#8220;They really bet on me the way Troy <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0EpgSowQTA0">bet on it</a>&#8221;, I said, only half jokingly. </p><p>Being taken seriously is a powerful feeling. And maybe, just maybe, there will be a day when I finally stop feeling like a tired, shiny race horse, constantly running forward, performing perfection in order to be bet on. </p><p><strong>Thursday, Aug 31. </strong></p><p>I had to cut my residency short by a day because I had to move. I&#8217;m trying to cook everything I have in the fridge. There&#8217;s so much left to do. Even though just a day prior I thought I had done everything, there&#8217;s always more hopes and dreams and regrets. I thought about how my mom used to say I could only enjoy half of our trips whenever we travelled because I spend the first 2 days unwilling to go and the last 2 days unwilling to return. Maybe this inertia is why I always have a hard time moving on.</p><p>10 minutes was the time I had. After I packed my bag and cleaned the house. Before the car came to pick me up to the train station. 10 minutes left &#8212; I could probably still browse one book. Listen to one radio. Eat some more food. Run to look at <a href="https://wavefarm.org/ta/archive/works/87eejz">Pond Station</a> one last time. </p><p>I thought about all these possibilities in my head, and sat down right where I was, legs brushing through the grass. Sunlight spreading evenly and generously on my body.</p><p>I know the answer is not a hut in the woods. But what if it somehow is? Not <em>THE</em> grand answer to anything specific. Just partially, insignificantly, some small answers to some bigger, unanswerable things. Like how I can remember things now. Like how I&#8217;m not afraid to leave. Like how 10 minutes can make such a difference sometimes.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><img src="/images/blogs/substack/the-answer-may-or-may-not-be-in-the/0650cf4e-c1ee-4ef6-bfc2-46f4a0cd4d72_3024x4032.jpeg" alt="" loading="lazy" ><figcaption class="image-caption">:,)</figcaption></figure></div><p>Thanks for reading this far. Happy fall. Take care! Talk to you soon!</p><p>Ok bye (&#8270;&#8269;&#820;&#795;&#7447;&#8269;&#820;&#795;&#8270;) &#10023;</p>
